DETECTORS:
LIGO: (Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory) There are two in the United States (Hanford, Washington, and Livingston, Louisiana). The arms are 4 km long.
Virgo: Gravitational-wave detector near Pisa, Italy, with 3-km-long arms.
KAGRA: Gravitational-wave detector in Kamioka, Japan, with 3-km-long arms underground.
LVK: The combined collaboration of LIGO, Virgo, and KAGRA.
LSC: (LIGO Scientific Collaboration) The international community of scientists working on LIGO.
IGWN: (International Gravitational-Wave Observatory) The framework for coordinating gravitational-wave observatories, including shared computing and services.
IceCube: A neutrino observatory at the South Pole. The sensors are embedded in roughly a cubic kilometer of ice to detect light produced by particles created in neutrino interactions.

SOURCES: 
GRB: (Gamma-ray burst) A brief, intense flash of gamma rays from a cosmic event. Many short GRBs come from compact-object mergers (e.g., black holes, neutron stars, and white dwarfs), whereas many long GRBs come from collapsing massive stars.
FRB: (Fast radio burst) A very short, bright burst of radio waves, typically lasting milliseconds (sometimes in repeats). Magnetars, i.e., highly magnetized neutron stars, can produce FRBs, but the origins of the full population remain under study.
Neutrino: A neutrino is an electrically neutral elementary particle with spin ½, belonging to the lepton family. Neutrinos interact through the weak nuclear force and gravity. Their interaction probabilities are extremely small, so they can traverse enormous amounts of matter without being absorbed or scattered.
Astrophysical neutrinos are produced through:
  • Nuclear fusion and beta processes: Reactions in stars produce neutrinos, including the electron neutrinos generated by hydrogen fusion in the Sun.
  • Core-collapse supernovae: Electron capture and thermal processes generate a large flux of neutrinos and antineutrinos, which carry away most of the energy released during the formation of a neutron star.
  • High-energy particle interactions: Accelerated protons or nuclei collide with surrounding matter or radiation, producing particles such as charged pions. Their decay chains generate neutrinos.
Because neutrinos interact weakly, they can escape optically thick regions (light cannot pass through easily since photons repeatedly scatter or get absorbed). This escape is not guaranteed: sufficiently dense matter can also trap neutrinos, as occurs temporarily in a newly formed neutron star. Their electrical neutrality also means that cosmic magnetic fields do not deflect their trajectories, making high-energy neutrinos useful for tracing their astrophysical origins. This is why, in multimessenger astronomy, neutrinos carry information from the source (astrophysical object or event that produced it). Detection relies on a rare interaction with matter. In IceCube, a neutrino can interact with a nucleus in the ice and produce charged secondary particles. These particles emit Cherenkov radiation when they travel faster than the speed of light in ice, while remaining slower than light in a vacuum. The recorded light pattern allows scientists to reconstruct the interaction and estimate the neutrino’s energy and arrival direction.

CODE:
PyCBC: Open-source, Python-based toolkit for detecing and analyzing gravitational waves from compact binary coalescences. It compares data with predicted signals and helps infer the sources' properties.
PyGRB: Gravitational-wave search pipeline within the PyCBC toolkit. It searches for compact binary merger signals near the time and sky position of an observed GRB.
PyNu: Multimessenger analysis built on PyCBC to search for associated gravitational-wave and neutrino events.
CIT: (California Institute of Technology) Usually, it refers to LIGO's computing resources hosted at Caltech.
OSG: (Open Science-Grid) It is a distributed computing infrastructure that lets researchers run large numbers of analysis jobs.

RUNS: Observing runs are periods when the detectors collect scientific data (# for run, letter for subdivision).
MMT: (Multimessenger Transients group) 
O3a/O3b: The two parts of the third observing run: O3a (April 1 - October 1, 2019), O3b (November 1, 2019 - March 27, 2020).
O4a/O4b/O4c: The three parts of the fourth observing run: O4a (May 24, 2023 - January 16, 2024), O4b (April 10, 2024 - January 28, 2025), O4c (January 28 - November 18, 2025 with break during April-June).
IR1: (Intermediate Run 1) An observing run planned between O4 and O5, providing another period of observations during the broader detector-upgrade program.

RESULTS:
DCC: (Document Control Center) LIGO's repository for papers, technical notes, presentations, etc. 
GW170817: The gravitational-wave event detected on August 17, 2017: the first observed gravitational-wave signal from a binary neutron-star merger. Its associated gamma-ray burst and other light made it a landmark multimessenger event.
GW150914: The gravitational-wave event detected on September 14, 2015: the first direct detection of gravitational waves, produced by two merging black holes.
CBC: (Compact binary coalescence) The spiraling and merging of two dense cosmic objects, such as black holes or neutron stars, that emit powerful gravitational waves. It includes BBH, BNS/NSNS, and NSBH mergers:
  • BBH: Binary black hole.
  • BNS/NSNS: Binary neutron star.
  • NSBH: Netron star-black hole.
